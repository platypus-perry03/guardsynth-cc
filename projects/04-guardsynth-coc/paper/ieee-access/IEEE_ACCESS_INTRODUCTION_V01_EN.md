# I. Introduction

Vision-language-action (VLA) driving models increasingly generate natural-language reasoning alongside driving plans. [Alpamayo-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00088), [DriveLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14150), [DriveVLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12289). Reasoning, however, does not guarantee acceptable behavior. An analysis of 150 reasoning–trajectory pairs from Alpamayo 1.5 judged 33.3% of the reasoning traces unreliable and found 74% trajectory agreement even for reliable ones. [Drive the Thoughts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29583). A further gap remains when reasoning and trajectory do agree: behavior must also satisfy externally specified constraints, such as traffic rules or operator instructions, that the model's own explanation need not encode. Chain of Causation (CoC) in Alpamayo-R1 illustrates this gap. [Alpamayo-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00088). CoC explains why observations lead to a decision, as in "a pedestrian is crossing, so the vehicle slows down." A constraint such as "do not enter while the zone is occupied, and wait 1.5 s after it clears" instead restricts which actions are admissible. Because the same scene admits different entry times under different waiting rules, a correct explanation cannot establish compliance.

Simply stating the rule to the model is not sufficient. When we supplied explicit speed limits to Alpamayo-R1-10B at inference, choosing limits that maintaining the current speed would satisfy, predictions still exceeded them within two seconds in 6/9 PhysicalAI and 9/9 converted nuScenes outputs, and raising the limit shifted predicted positions by at most 0.087 m on average. When asked to hold position within 0.2 m for two seconds, all three predictions for a low-speed input from each source left that radius. The violation counts were the same whether the rule was given in the user prompt or appended to the CoC.[^1] Because these diagnostics do not test training, we examined it separately in a controlled synthetic candidate-selection task with a smaller VLM, Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct. In a position-hold task, a CoC-only model given the rule only at evaluation violated it in 22/192 predictions, whereas models that also received constraints during training violated it in 1/192 and 0/192.[^2] Constraints therefore need to enter the training data. This raises a further question: are the constraints themselves correct?

Answering this question is difficult for three reasons, and existing approaches address only parts of it. First, a single constraint combines evidence with different roles: an observed clearance time, a supplied waiting duration, and an assumption that the zone stays clear. When such a constraint is wrong, a natural-language sentence alone does not reveal which source is at fault. Second, errors are silent but consequential. Constraints that read correctly but were attached to the wrong training cases left accuracy near 50%, the constraint-free level, and small changes in units, negation, boundaries, or release conditions alter admissible behavior. Third, checking requires a formal representation, whereas a VLM learns from natural language, and the learned interpretation depends on wording: rewording the constraints without changing their meaning reduced static-task accuracy of the CNL-trained model from 100% to 80.32% (Section VI). A checked meaning is therefore useful for learning only if it is rendered consistently. Reasoning frameworks such as DriveLM and DriveVLM do not represent admissibility constraints separately from reasoning. [DriveLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14150), [DriveVLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12289). Reachability-based safety filters restrict control inputs at execution rather than shaping training data. [Context-conditioned HJ reachability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08115). nl2spec translates requirements into temporal logic but neither binds them to scene evidence nor regenerates checked learning inputs. [nl2spec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04864). Our earlier natural-language guards improved action selection but left their construction unchecked.

We present GuardSynth, a development method that addresses each of these difficulties. (i) It binds constraint fields to their sources, separating observations, supplied rules, and modeling assumptions. (ii) It records applicability, action restrictions, persistence and release, timing, and evidence in Evidence-Bound Lifecycle Contracts (EBLC). Supported profiles are lowered to SMT to check consistency and designated properties within a restricted model. (iii) It generates Controlled Natural Language (CNL) from the checked fields and appends it to the original CoC, so the model still learns from language. For real scenes, the required observations and applicability need human confirmation.

We evaluate Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct on six synthetic action-selection tasks with matched LoRA budgets and three seeds. Both natural-language constraints (P1) and EBLC-derived CNL (P2) outperform CoC alone (P0) in accuracy and violation rate. On temporal entry, accuracy is 49.86%, 96.81%, and 98.06%, and violation rate is 19.17%, 1.39%, and 0.83%. The behavioral gain stems mainly from supplying constraints, and P2 does not outperform P1 under every condition. What GuardSynth adds is checkability: its checks detect all 144 injected errors of six supported types and accept all 24 valid contracts. They miss 24 errors in which evidence and specification are wrong together, which marks the limit of internal consistency checks.

Our contributions are:

1. A procedure for selecting and binding supplied behavioral constraints in CoC context while separating observations, rules, and modeling assumptions.
2. An EBLC-based path from evidence-bound specifications through restricted logical verification to CNL inserted into CoC.
3. An evaluation that separates model compliance and selection from specification quality across six behavioral tasks.
